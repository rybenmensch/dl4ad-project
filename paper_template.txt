\begin{abstract}
This paper presents \texttt{nbform}, an exploratory tool for artistic manipulation of pretrained neural audio models. The system exposes model components through a common interface and applies structural or parameter interventions without retraining. A reproducible parameter-sweep workflow compares each modified reconstruction with an unmodified baseline and summarizes the resulting intervention space in a heatmap. An EnCodec evaluation across four recordings demonstrates that the measured response depends on layer, operation, parameter value, and input. The results are a technical orientation for selecting material for listening-based exploration, not a user study or a measure of artistic quality.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% no \IEEEPARstart
Deep generative models produce audio by learning a data distribution, but the same optimization for fidelity can make their behaviour appear uniform. For artistic practice, the model can instead be treated as material for exploration: its internal operations, learned parameters, and failure modes become possible sites of intervention.

Network bending applies this logic to neural networks \cite{broad_network_2021}. Inspired by circuit bending \cite{ghazala_folk_2004}, it treats a trained model as a modifiable system whose graph and parameters can be altered to produce unexpected outputs. The goal is not to improve reconstruction fidelity, but to make the model's internal structure available as an artistic instrument.

Network bending has been demonstrated in individual architectures, including neural vocoders based on a DDSP model \cite{yee-king_studio_2021} and the VAE model RAVE \cite{oliveira_brave_2025}. Existing implementations are typically coupled to a specific model. Applying the same bending techniques to a different architecture can be challenging, as the internal structure and naming conventions of neural audio models vary between implementations.
 
This work presents \texttt{nbform}, a model-adaptive tool for exploratory network bending of pretrained neural audio models. Rather than introducing a new transformation technique, it separates model-specific access from reusable structural and parameter interventions, allowing the same intervention classes to be applied across supported architectures. We further provide an illustrative characterization of isolated interventions in the 48-kHz EnCodec model across selected encoder and decoder layers and four recordings. The resulting heatmap describes measured intervention sensitivity and provides candidate configurations for subsequent listening-based exploration; it does not measure artistic quality or prove improved artistic interaction.

\subsection{Related Work}
Network bending has been applied to neural vocoders and RAVE to induce active divergence from a model's expected behaviour \cite{yee-king_studio_2021,oliveira_brave_2025}. Existing tools are commonly tied to one architecture; \texttt{nbform} places model-specific access behind a shared interface while keeping interventions reusable. EnCodec provides the main evaluation case because its encoder--quantized-latent--decoder structure makes layer-level interventions explicit \cite{defossez_high_2022}.

\subsubsection{Network bending Framework}
Broad et al. \cite{broad_network_2021} introduced the notion of network bending as a direct manipulation of deep generative models to induce ``active divergence'' in the model's output relative to the original training distribution. They propose a method where a set of transformations can be inserted as distinct layers into the computational graph during inference. Thus, there is no modification of the model's learned weights.
In contrast, Kotowski et al. \cite{kotowski_network_2026} propose to intervene directly in the model's parameter space, introducing a network bending tool that enables real-time manipulation of the weights and biases of neural audio models. Manz et al. \cite{oliveira_brave_2025} propose a hardware implementation of the same approach applied to RAVE, which results in a network bending instrument. McCallum and Yee-King \cite{yee-king_studio_2021} developed an exploratory toolkit that focuses on transformations of a DDSP-based vocoder.

From a purely machine-learning perspective, these approaches produce undesirable results because they actively depart from the model's intended function. From a creative perspective, however, they treat malfunction and unintended use as artistic resources. This aligns with Cascone's account of glitch aesthetics \cite{cascone_aesthetics_2000}, in which composers seek digital failures as material for composition.

Beyond artistic use, network bending has political and pedagogical implications: pretrained models can be explored without the computational cost of training or extensive prior knowledge. As Kotowski et al. describe it, network bending enables ``exploratory intervention and repurposing'' \cite{kotowski_network_2026}.


\subsubsection{Audio model context}
RAVE is a real-time waveform synthesizer based on a variational autoencoder, whereas EnCodec is a neural audio codec with a convolutional encoder, residual vector quantization, and decoder \cite{caillonRAVEVariationalAutoencoder2021,defossez_high_2022}. The adapters expose these different architectures through the same intervention workflow; only EnCodec is used for the quantitative evaluation below.

\section{Technical Design and Implementation}
The tool is designed for artistic exploration rather than optimization toward a target signal. It lets users inspect a pretrained model, select encoder or decoder components, apply and combine interventions, and export outputs for listening. The workflow separates model access, interventions, inference, and result export across supported architectures.

\subsection{Model and Layer Abstraction}
\label{model_layer_abstraction}
Architecture-specific adapters implement a shared \texttt{NNModel} interface. They expose encoder and decoder containers, model audio requirements, layer metadata, and supported weight and bias tensors. A direct child of an exposed container is an intervention target and is identified by a stable path and a \texttt{LayerInfo} record. Composite targets apply parameter operations to all supported tensors exposed inside the component. RAVE requires its training checkpoint and \texttt{config.gin}; EnCodec uses its public pretrained 24~kHz or 48~kHz model.

\subsubsection{Transformation Operators}
\label{transformation_operators}
Four operators probe complementary affordances: computation can be omitted (\texttt{skip}), repeated for $r\in\{3,5,10\}$ (\texttt{repeat}), weights and biases can be scaled (\texttt{multiply}), or shifted by an offset (\texttt{add}). For a selected component $f_l$ with input $z$, skipping uses $\widetilde{f}_l(z)=z$ and repetition uses $\widetilde{f}_l(z)=f_l^r(z)$. Parameter operations use
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde{W}=\alpha W,\quad \widetilde{b}=\beta b,
  \qquad\text{or}\qquad
  \widetilde{W}=W+\gamma,\quad \widetilde{b}=b+\eta.
\end{equation}
The automated sweep selects shape-compatible candidates; a failed shape or runtime check is recorded rather than terminating the sweep.

\subsubsection{Reproducible Sweep}
For technical characterization, candidate targets are direct children of the exposed encoder and decoder containers. A candidate is retained when the adapter reports matching input and output channels or no channel information; the evaluated EnCodec model yielded ten positions per section. This accessible set is not an exhaustive analysis of all internal operations. Each trial starts from a pristine model, tests factors $\{1,5,10,20\}$ or an additive offset of 0.1, and stores its layer, parameters, status, audio, and metrics in CSV format.

Let $y_i$ and $\widehat{y}_i$ be sample $i$ of the baseline reconstruction and transformed output, respectively. For $N$ waveform samples, mean absolute error is
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{MAE}(y,\widehat{y})
    = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\widehat{y}_i-y_i\right|.
\end{equation}
Output changes are also measured using multi-resolution short-time Fourier transform loss, which compares spectrograms computed with different analysis resolutions \cite{yamamoto_parallel_2020}. MR-STFT is retained as a secondary spectral descriptor; the heatmap and the present interpretation focus on normalized MAE. These measures characterize waveform and spectral deviations from the baseline reconstruction, not perceptual quality or artistic usefulness. The heatmap characterizes first-order effects of isolated interventions and does not predict interaction effects from combined transformations.

\subsection{Heatmap as an Exploratory Tool}
The heatmap provides a candidate view for subsequent listening. Its left panel aggregates normalized MAE by layer and intervention setting, while the right panel provides a complementary view of multi-resolution spectral deviation through MR-STFT. Configurations with greater measured response are made visible for further exploration; the figure does not identify artistically promising regions, rank perceptual preference, or demonstrate improved interaction. Black borders mark clipped cells, and blank cells indicate only untested combinations.

\section{Method}
The exemplar analysis used the pretrained 48-kHz EnCodec model and four recordings: \textit{AmenBreak.wav}, \textit{Fontane Sprachsignal.wav}, \textit{GLM.wav}, and \textit{tof.wav}. Direct child modules in the encoder and decoder were tested, producing ten accessible candidate positions per network section. The sweep evaluated \texttt{skip}, \texttt{repeat} with 3, 5, or 10 repetitions, \texttt{multiply} with factors 1, 5, 10, or 20, and \texttt{add} with an offset of 0.1. Each trial began from the same pretrained model and was compared with its unmodified reconstruction. Results were summarized descriptively over the four recordings using normalized MAE, secondary MR-STFT values, and quality-gate status.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

The evaluation used the pretrained 48-kHz EnCodec model \cite{defossez_high_2022} and four recordings: \textit{AmenBreak.wav}, \textit{Fontane Sprachsignal.wav}, \textit{GLM.wav}, and \textit{tof.wav}. The adapter converted each input to EnCodec's required sample rate and channel configuration. The environment used Python $\geq$3.11, PyTorch $\geq$2.2, EnCodec 0.1.1, auraloss $\geq$0.4, and librosa $\geq$0.11. RAVE is supported by the project but not quantitatively evaluated here.

The evaluation is an illustrative application of the tool rather than a perceptual or user study. It comprised 480 isolated-intervention trials across 20 accessible EnCodec layer positions and four recordings. Each trial was reset to the pretrained model and compared with the corresponding unmodified reconstruction. Normalized MAE was used for the sensitivity map; MR-STFT was retained as a secondary spectral descriptor. The results identify configurations with different measured responses for subsequent listening, not artistic quality.

A quality gate recorded non-finite outputs and near-silence, and flagged clipping when more than 5\% of samples reached $|x|\geq0.99$; clipped outputs were retained and interpreted as boundary cases. For normalized MAE, baseline and intervention outputs were normalized independently to RMS 0.1 and peak-limited to 0.99 before comparison. Consequently, normalized MAE emphasizes waveform differences after independent level alignment; raw MAE preserves unnormalized signal differences. Results were exported per trial and summarized by layer, operation, and parameter setting using medians and interquartile ranges. The four recordings are the independent stimuli; intervention trials are repeated measurements, not independent observations. No user study was conducted.


\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results-discussion}

\subsection{Corpus-Level Results}

The evaluation comprised 480 trials across four recordings and 20 accessible EnCodec layer positions: 80 skip, 240 repeat, 128 multiply, and 32 additive conditions. Of these, 478 were valid and two were clipped; both clipped cases occurred for skipping \texttt{decoder.model.13}. The recordings are the stimulus set, not 480 independent observations.

Across the tested conditions, the median normalized MAE was 0.0264 for \texttt{skip}, 0.0319 for \texttt{repeat}, and 0.0219 for \texttt{add}. Non-control weight multiplications produced a median normalized MAE of 0.0053 across factors 5, 10, and 20; their factor-specific medians were 0.0050, 0.0053, and 0.0054. The factor-1 condition was retained as an unchanged control and is reported separately in the heatmap. These are descriptive summaries of the tested configurations; no inferential significance is claimed.

\subsection{Layer and Stimulus Dependence}

Among the tested layer positions, the strongest median deviations included skipping \texttt{decoder.model.13} (normalized MAE 0.1057) and repeating \texttt{encoder.model.4} ten times (0.1025). The largest individual normalized MAE was 0.1276 for \texttt{decoder.model.10} with five repetitions on \textit{GLM.wav}; the largest skip value was 0.1233 for \texttt{encoder.model.4} on the same recording. These cases show that the measured response varied with target layer, intervention setting, and input.

The two clipped outputs were specific to the skip intervention at \texttt{decoder.model.13}; they are treated as boundary cases and do not establish a general tendency for decoder interventions to clip. Because clipping can contribute substantially to waveform deviation, these cases are reported separately when interpreting the largest observed MAE values. Likewise, the comparatively high values for \textit{GLM.wav} describe this stimulus set and do not identify a causal spectral or temporal property of that recording.

\subsection{Implications for Artistic Exploration}

Figure~\ref{fig:intervention-heatmap} shows that the measured response varies among the tested layer/intervention pairs. The sweep therefore provides a descriptive sensitivity map from which configurations can be selected for closer listening. It characterizes isolated interventions rather than the interaction space of combined transformations.

MAE and MR-STFT quantify signal differences, however, and do not determine whether an output is aesthetically interesting or useful. The heatmap is therefore an intermediate representation between model analysis and artistic exploration: it structures part of the search space and provides candidate configurations for subsequent listening. Whether this information improves the efficiency, controllability, or quality of artistic interaction remains an open question for future user-centred evaluation.

\section{Conclusion and Outlook}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\subsection{Conclusion}

This paper presented \texttt{nbform}, a model-adaptive tool for exploratory network bending of pretrained neural audio models. Its model abstraction separates architecture-specific access to encoder and decoder components from reusable structural and parameter interventions. The quantitative characterization reported here is an illustrative EnCodec case study covering four recordings and isolated interventions at accessible layer positions.

The tested interventions produced heterogeneous reconstruction deviations across layer positions, parameter settings, and input recordings. Non-control weight multiplications produced a median normalized MAE of 0.0053, while skip and repeat conditions showed larger deviations in the tested configuration. These observations characterize the evaluated EnCodec model, stimuli, and interventions; they do not establish perceptual preference, artistic value, or general behavior across models and inputs.

The sensitivity map provides an intermediate representation between model analysis and artistic exploration. It can structure candidate selection for subsequent listening, while aesthetic interpretation remains part of the user's practice. Whether this information improves the efficiency, controllability, or quality of artistic interaction remains open for future user-centred evaluation.

\subsection{Outlook}

Future work should expand the stimulus set, evaluate RAVE with a comparable protocol, and pair the sensitivity map with structured listening studies. Further development could investigate finer structural interventions and interactions between combined transformations.
